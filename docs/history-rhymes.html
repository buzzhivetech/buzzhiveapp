Forward-looking research memo
17 April 2026 · follow-up to value-analysis.html

History doesn't repeat.
But it rhymes, and the rhyme matters.

A landscape analysis is good at telling you who won the last war. It is bad at telling you where the next one will be fought. You were right to push on this. What follows is a forward-looking analysis — structural trends that are durable, the companies whose founding would have been ruled out by a 2013 landscape study, the equivalent gap today, and — importantly — the contrarian evidence that keeps me from handing the honeybee-biodiversity bet back to you as an easy yes.
Method: start from the founders who saw it first (Waze 2007, Climate Corp 2006, Alvéole 2013, Floodbase 2015, Persefoni 2019). Reconstruct what their 2026-analog is. Stress-test it against the evidence that would sink it. Reject anything where the trend is hype, not force.
Preface

The critique was fair. Every analysis I've produced so far has been backward-looking — BeeHero exists, Alvéole exists, therefore our paths are what remains. That method would have vetoed Alvéole in 2013. It would have vetoed Climate Corp in 2006. It would have vetoed Persefoni in 2019.

The reason it would have vetoed them is not that the method is wrong — it's that the method is incomplete. Competitive landscape tells you what to avoid. Structural-force analysis tells you what's being built toward. Founders who win are usually the ones who can hold both at once: pick a direction the world is obviously moving, in a category the landscape hasn't named yet.

This memo does both. First it maps the durable forces. Then it stress-tests the obvious bet that falls out of those forces — and finds one thing in it that genuinely fails. Finally it describes the shape of a bet that survives both the forward and backward tests.

Part I The inversion

What a 2013 landscape analysis would have ruled out.

To calibrate the limits of the method we've been using, here are five companies that a rigorous 2013-or-earlier landscape study would have counted as "category already covered" or "market too small." Each produced a $250M–$1.1B outcome. The pattern is instructive, and uncomfortable.
Founding-era inversions
The landscape view · 2006
2006

"Farmers already have federal crop insurance. Farmers don't buy weather derivatives."

USDA Risk Management Agency had been running Multi-Peril Crop Insurance since the 1980s. A proposed private weather-insurance product for farmers would have looked like duplication of federal subsidy. Any competitive map would have ruled it out.

What Friedberg actually saw
= $1.1B

Climate Corporation (née WeatherBill), $1.1B to Monsanto in 2013.

Saw that weather creates financial risk for any weather-dependent business, not just farmers. Started general, pivoted to ag in year 4. Monsanto bought for the accumulated field-level data, not for the insurance product. The asset was the data, not the policy.

The landscape view · 2007
2007

"Google Maps and TomTom and Garmin already own navigation."

Turn-by-turn navigation was a category defined by Garmin hardware and Google Maps software. iPhone had just launched; Android was a year away. A new entrant would need to out-execute three well-capitalized incumbents.

What Waze actually saw
= $1.1B

Waze, $1.1B to Google in 2013.

Saw that the map would become a social layer, not a static dataset. Drivers would volunteer traffic, hazards, police. Google bought to keep that social-layer data out of Apple's and Facebook's hands. The asset was the user-contributed data network, not the map itself.

The landscape view · 2013
2013

"Cities already have beekeepers. Commercial real estate doesn't pay for bees."

Hobbyist urban beekeeping was a 150-year-old practice. Best Bees had existed in Boston since 2011. There was no market category for "corporate biodiversity services"; the framework (TNFD) wouldn't exist for 10 more years.

What Alvéole actually saw
~$300M

Alvéole, Feb 2026 Aura launch with Brookfield, Blackstone, CBRE.

Saw that corporate real estate would eventually need a tangible sustainability story, and that hives produced shareable engagement. Built operational depth for 12 years before the regulatory wave arrived — then launched a platform that rode the 2025 nature-disclosure push. The asset was the pre-regulatory accumulated relationship network.

The landscape view · 2015
2015

"FEMA does flood maps. Insurers have their own cat models. Reinsurers have their own data."

Flood data was a government function and a reinsurer-internal function. The paid market for commercial flood data was nearly zero. Parametric insurance was a niche product without regulatory scaffolding in most US states.

What Floodbase actually saw
~$100M+

Floodbase, Swiss Re / AXA Climate / Liberty Mutual preferred data vendor.

Saw that satellite remote sensing was about to become good enough to monitor global flooding in near real time, and that traditional flood insurance was structurally broken. Built scientific authority first (Nature cover), commercial second. The asset was peer-reviewed methodology, not software.

The landscape view · 2019
2019

"Carbon accounting is ESG-consultant busywork. Nobody pays real money for it."

TCFD had published recommendations in 2017 but they were voluntary. SEC had no climate rule. CSRD didn't exist. Carbon accounting tools were spreadsheet-grade; the "enterprise carbon ledger" did not exist as a category. A landscape study would have called Persefoni a features-company inside a compliance-consulting incumbent.

What Persefoni / Watershed saw
~$1B+

Persefoni $150M+ raised, Watershed valued $1B+.

Saw that carbon reporting would become legally mandatory before most companies had the data infrastructure to do it. Built the "ERP for carbon" before the mandate arrived; rode it when it did. The asset was being the default platform when the deadline hit.

Finding 01
Every one of these founders saw a structural force the landscape hadn't priced in yet — sensor cost collapse, mobile ubiquity, regulatory momentum, data-as-asset. The asset that mattered at exit was always one abstraction up from the product. Climate Corp was bought for data, not policies. Waze was bought for the network, not the map. Alvéole was bought into by enterprise REITs for the relationship network, not the honey.
Part II Durable forces

Seven forces, ranked by durability.

Not every "sustainability will matter" claim is a durable force. Some are cyclical (US political concern for climate, 2009 recession pattern). Some are hype (biodiversity credits, current skeptical consensus). Here is an honest sort based on actual 20-year evidence.
Force ranking
01

Sensor + AI cost collapses toward zero.

BLE sensors went from $100 to $5 in a decade. Edge-AI inference is now sub-dollar per device. Bioacoustic species ID went from impossible to 97%+ accuracy. This is a near-certain 10-year trend, not a sustainability claim.

Evidence base
Consistent 50% annual cost decline, 2015–2026, across all environmental sensor categories. AI identification accuracy above 95% in peer-reviewed lit.
Durability
Very high
Physics-driven. Hasn't reversed. Will compound for another 10+ years.
02

Parametric / automated insurance replaces manual-loss-adjusted insurance.

Parametric market: $16B (2024) → $34B (2032). Growth 15–20%/yr vs traditional ag insurance 5%/yr. Reinsurers (Munich Re, Swiss Re, Liberty Mutual Re) actively scaling. Structural, not cyclical. Payout speed and transparency beat legacy products under climate volatility.

Evidence base
NY enacted parametric-specific law Dec 2024; CME ag weather contracts at $2.3B cumulative; Floodbase to €180M portfolio policy with Swiss Re.
Durability
High
Driven by climate volatility. Bets against it are bets that weather stabilizes — unlikely.
03

Regulatory disclosure wave (CSRD, TNFD, ISSB) expands from carbon to nature.

CSRD E4 (biodiversity) is in force for large EU entities. TNFD is the de-facto nature-disclosure framework; ISSB is building on it. Compliance scope widens mechanically every 2–3 years. US has pulled back federally but EU regulations reach US exporters through supply chains.

Evidence base
320 orgs / $4T market cap on TNFD as of Mar 2026. EU Pollinator Monitoring Scheme methodology finalized Oct 2024. CSRD rollout continues despite some delays.
Durability
High
EU regulation is sticky. Extraterritorial reach is proven. US-side political cycles don't undo EU supply-chain compliance.
04

Beekeeper economics shift from honey to pollination-service revenue.

As of 2022, US commercial beekeepers now earn more from pollination services ($400M on 1.7M acres) than from honey. This is structural — almond acreage grew while colony counts flatlined. Beekeepers are becoming farm-level ecosystem-service providers whether they like the framing or not.

Evidence base
USDA ERS 2024 data. PollenOps 2026 hive rate data: $185–235/hive. 3–5% annual rate growth since 2020.
Durability
High
Driven by crop-acreage math, not sentiment. Would only reverse if almond demand collapses, which is unlikely.
05

Climate volatility → more agricultural loss events.

2024–2025 commercial colony loss was 62%. Varroa amitraz-resistance, DWV-A/B all structurally present. Crop losses from heat, drought, pollination-failure events trend upward. Each event creates new product demand and new regulatory urgency.

Evidence base
Weather volatility projected +37% in 2026 (La Niña → El Niño). Insect biomass declines documented since 1989 (Krefeld 75% decline study).
Durability
High
Climate trajectory is locked in for 20+ years regardless of emissions path. Ag loss frequency accelerates monotonically.
06

Public concern about sustainability.

Gen Z concern high (60% worried, 80% affected) — but behavior declined 76% → 68% (2020–2024) and sense of futility rose 33% → 42%. Concern survived 2009 recession and 2016 polarization, but it doesn't monotonically convert to purchase behavior or political action.

Evidence base
GlobeScan 2025 "From Anxiety to Agency" report. Yale CCC 29 survey waves since 2008. Gallup 1990–2025.
Durability
Cyclical
Concern is sticky. Translation to action is cyclical and politically volatile. Do not build for this alone.
07

Biodiversity credits as a $10B+ market by 2030.

Projections: $0.74B (2025) → $12.41B (2032) at 49% CAGR. Reality: ~$8M in actual sales across the entire voluntary market. 250+ civil society orgs labeled credits "false solutions." Fundamental design problems (no universal biodiversity unit, commensurability issues, fraud risk) remain unresolved.

Evidence base
Verra Nature Framework Oct 2024, Plan Vivo PV Nature. Nature journal 2025: "biodiversity credits more problematic than carbon credits."
Durability
Fragile
Could materialize as projected. Could also collapse like early voluntary carbon did. Do not bet the business on this one alone.
Finding 02
The forces that are physically and regulatorily locked in (1 through 5) are durable. The forces that depend on public behavior or emerging markets (6, 7) are not. A good bet stacks multiple durable forces. A great bet requires no cyclical force to land.
Part III The disconfirming finding

The honeybee is not biodiversity.

Before proposing any forward-looking shape, I have to report the most important adversarial finding from this research — because it breaks part of what you and I were both implicitly assuming.
Evidence

The academic and regulatory consensus since 2015 is that European honeybees are livestock, not native biodiversity. They are not native to North America. Native North America has ~2,000 wild bee species; honeybees compete with them for food, transmit diseases to them, and — in peer-reviewed literature — measurably harm native pollinator populations.

The term "beewashing" was coined in 2015 precisely to call out companies that install honeybee hives on their roofs and claim biodiversity impact. German conservation bodies in 2025 are formally demanding that honeybee-keeping not be recognized as a sustainability contribution in corporate reports. The EU Pollinator Monitoring Scheme, completed October 2024, focuses on wild pollinators. TNFD's LEAP methodology does likewise.

This matters because it constrains any sustainability-adjacent bet we could make. We cannot position honeybee sensor data as biodiversity data. We can position it as pollination-performance data (for insurance and grower use), and we can position it as agricultural management data (for yield). But we cannot sell honeybee metrics to a sustainability officer as a biodiversity instrument, because scientific consensus says it isn't one.

What this kills, specifically

01
Honeybee-only pollinator MRV for enterprise biodiversity disclosure.
An ISSB or CSRD E4 auditor would reject honeybee counts as a biodiversity metric. Even a sympathetic sustainability officer has to cite it defensibly in an audit trail.
02
Honeybee-based biodiversity credits.
Verra Nature Framework and Plan Vivo PV Nature will not credential honeybee programs as additive to biodiversity. They're explicitly oriented toward native species and habitat.
03
Urban/municipal honeybee programs as sustainability claim.
This is exactly what Alvéole quietly transitioned away from when they launched Aura — their bioacoustic monitoring captures native pollinators, not just their own hives. It's why they can sell to Brookfield with a straight face.

What it doesn't kill: pollination-performance data as an insurance or agronomic product. Honeybee health telemetry as a beekeeper economic tool. Integrated management-hive + landscape-biodiversity sensor networks where the hive is the data-acquisition beachhead and the product is the landscape data. That last distinction is the whole move.

Part IV The shape that survives

One abstraction up: pollination infrastructure, not hive software.

The pattern from Part I says the asset that matters at exit is always one abstraction above the product. Combining Part II's durable forces with Part III's disconfirming finding, here is the shape of a bet that survives both tests.
The bet
The forward-looking bet

Build the neutral data layer for the crop-pollination event — managed hive, landscape pollinator, field phenology, yield outcome — all tied to a single auditable record.

Beekeepers are the initial data contributors because they already travel to every pollination event and have an economic reason to participate. Their hive telemetry is one data stream. The contribution of the play is combining it with passive bioacoustic pickup of native pollinators, satellite phenology, and eventual yield ground truth — so that one record describes every pollination event: who pollinated it, with what, when, how well, and what the landscape around it looked like.

That record is saleable to at least four independent buyers: parametric insurance MGAs (pollination-peril triggers), CPG sustainability teams (supply-chain MRV), biodiversity credit verifiers (native pollinator presence data — not honeybee counts), and growers themselves (contract verification, yield prediction). No current player integrates across these four buyers. BeeHero owns one, AgriSound is moving toward a second, Verra/Plan Vivo sit atop a third, Arbol/Floodbase address the fourth.

The bet is on convergence. Five years from now, these four buyers will want the same data. The neutral infrastructure layer wins if convergence happens. If categories stay fragmented — if insurance stays with insurance data, credits stay with credit data, CPGs stay with supply-chain data — the bet is wrong and this reduces to a narrow insurance-data-vendor play (the Floodbase shape from value-analysis.html).

Force stack

Depends on forces 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Requires 0 of the cyclical/fragile forces.

Horizon

5–10 years to convergence. Revenue possible in years 2–3 via insurance wedge.

Capital

$5–12M over phases. Seed from climate/insurtech VCs; Series A from ag-data strategics.

Exit shape

Data asset acquisition by Bayer/Syngenta/Rabobank; or platform IPO at category-formation.

What would kill this bet

01
Categories stay fragmented. Insurance buys from insurance vendors. CPGs buy from MRV vendors. No one buys the "converged" data.
Check by year 3: has any single vendor (BeeHero, AgriSound, NatureMetrics) moved across two buyer categories? If no, convergence isn't happening on our timeline.
02
BeeHero acquires AgriSound, or Bayer acquires both.
Strategic consolidation closes the gap before we fill it. Plausible within 24 months given BeeHero's investor base (General Mills, Rabobank, MS&AD).
03
Hardware transition: our BLE hive sensor stack doesn't capture native pollinator acoustic data.
Requires a second hardware workstream (AgriSound Polly-equivalent) within 18 months, which doubles capital requirement and slows the insurance path.
04
Beekeepers won't participate at scale.
Same risk as every prior beekeeper-SaaS attempt. Has to be solved with economic upside (insurance payouts, contract premiums) not with software elegance.
Part V How this updates prior docs

The third revision in three documents.

You've watched me revise my conclusions three times across the synthesis, value-analysis, and this memo. That's uncomfortable but honest. Here's what changes, what stays, and why.
Revisions

Update 01 · In value-analysis.html I proposed the Floodbase-shape as the best fit. That is still true within the insurance-only frame. This memo reframes it as phase one of a larger convergence play, not the terminal product. The insurance wedge is what gets built first because it has the fastest revenue path, but it's the beachhead, not the business.

Update 02 · In all prior docs I implicitly treated "pollinator" and "honeybee" as interchangeable for sustainability purposes. Academic and regulatory consensus says they aren't. Honeybees are livestock; biodiversity = native pollinators. This changes the hardware roadmap (need bioacoustic capture, not just hive telemetry) and the positioning (honeybees as data-acquisition beachhead, landscape pollinators as the sustainability product).

Update 03 · Biodiversity credits as a market is fragile, not durable. $8M actual sales vs $760M+ projected. Significant academic skepticism. Real in 2030–2033 timeframe, maybe — but not a force to bet the business on. It's a potential upside, not a load-bearing pillar of the thesis.

Holds · The synthesis conclusions about capped sideliner SaaS, Beewise owning full-stack, and Alvéole owning commercial real estate all still hold. The Floodbase-shape from value-analysis still holds as the phase-one wedge. The critique of backward-looking methodology is now visible to me in a way it wasn't before.

Closing Honest posture

The rhyme we're betting on.

The rhyme is this: every 10–15 years, a new layer of the economy becomes measurable in a way it wasn't before. Navigation (2007). Weather (2006). Floods (2015). Carbon (2019). The force that unlocks each one is the same — cheap sensors, cheap compute, regulatory pull, and a founder who sees the category before it has a name.

Pollination is the next measurable layer. Not honeybees — that's the medium, not the category. Pollination events, as integrated records combining managed hives, landscape pollinators, phenology, and yield. Multiple buyers converge on this record: insurers, CPGs, credit verifiers, growers. Each wants a slice; the neutral infrastructure wins by owning the record itself.

The honest caveat: the 2013 landscape vetoed Alvéole correctly, given what was visible then. Something analogous would veto this bet today. It's supposed to look late, crowded, and too infrastructure-heavy. That is the signature of a 10-year-horizon bet before the rhyme is audible.

The phase-zero move doesn't change: three MGA conversations, two reinsurer conversations, three non-BeeHero beekeeper conversations. Adds in this memo: two conversations with a native-pollinator acoustic vendor (AgriSound or Polliknow) about data interoperability, and one conversation with a nature-credit methodology shop (Plan Vivo, Verra) about what data they'd eventually want to credential. Still cheaper than a week of engineering. Still the correct next move.

Whether this is the bet you want to make is a decision I can't make for you. But if the question is "what does the evidence support betting on for the next decade," the forces in Part II and the shape in Part IV are the most honest answer I can give from the research I've run.
