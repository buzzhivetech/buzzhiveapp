Six weeks. One decision.

This playbook is the operational companion to the board briefing. It contains the target contact list for each axis, outreach templates you can copy, diagnostic call scripts, scoring rubrics, and the week-six synthesis framework. Open this document every morning of Phase 0. Close it when the go/no-go memo is written.

How to use this document

01 · Cadence
One axis per two weeks, overlapping
Start the insurance axis immediately (week 1–2), beekeeper by end of week 1, biodiversity by week 3, consultancy by week 4. Synthesis week six. Do not wait for one axis to finish before starting the next — some contacts take 10 days to respond.
02 · Discipline
Log every call within two hours
Use the call-log template at the bottom of each axis. Fill it in while the conversation is fresh. A week later you will remember the vibe, not the signal. The whole plan is designed to produce evidence — don't waste it by forgetting what was said.
03 · Rigor
Ask the kill question every time
The last question on each script is explicitly designed to kill the thesis. Do not skip it because the conversation went well. A polite yes without the kill question is worthless data; a polite yes after the kill question is the only kind that counts.

Diagnostic script · 20 minutes · insurance axis

  1. Walk me through how your team thinks about agriculture parametric products today — what perils do you cover, what data sources trigger them, where are the gaps?Opens the conversation, lets them frame their world in their language. Don't lead with bees.
  2. Has pollination failure or bee colony loss ever come up as a peril worth writing? If yes, why hasn't it shipped; if no, what makes it outside your scope?Draws out whether the peril is on their radar at all. The "why not" answer is the most useful data.
  3. If a scientific-grade dataset existed — site-level, continuous, peer-reviewable methodology, combining managed hive telemetry with native-pollinator acoustic presence and landscape phenology — is that a data product you would license, co-develop, or not interesting?The core question. Don't leave it implicit. Force them to answer one of three.
  4. What rigor bar does data need to clear to be insurance-grade? Published methodology? Independent validation? Historical depth — three years, five years, ten?Calibrates the build cost on our side. Floodbase built on a Nature-cover methodology — what's our equivalent minimum?
  5. If we were structured correctly — the data passes your rigor bar, site coverage is national — what's the willingness-to-pay shape? Per-acre, per-policy, per-site, flat annual license, data-exchange?Don't ask "how much would you pay." Ask structure. Price comes later.
  6. Honest question — is there any version of pollination or bee-colony data you would seriously pay for in the next 24 months? If the answer is no, what would have to change for the answer to become yes?The kill question. A polite yes to earlier questions doesn't survive this one. The follow-up "what would change" surfaces real blockers, not theoretical ones.

Scoring rubric · insurance axis

Strong signal · pass
2+ MGAs say yes to Q3 and engage on Q5 (pricing structure) without being prompted. OR 1+ reinsurer says "let's talk about a pilot / co-development."
Weak signal · conditional
1+ MGA says yes to Q3 but requires a published methodology or 3+ years of data before buying. This is the Floodbase precedent — real but delays revenue by 24 months.
Kill · axis closed
0/3 MGAs express appetite in Q3 OR 3/3 answer Q6 with "no, we'd never pay for this — existing crop insurance and beekeeper contracts already hedge it." Drop the insurance leg of the thesis.

Diagnostic script · 20–30 minutes · beekeeper axis

  1. Walk me through your operation — how many hives, what verticals (pollination / honey / queens), what's your contract structure for pollination?Mandatory context. The right next question depends on whether they're 80% almond pollination or 80% honey.
  2. What data, if any, are you sharing with growers today? What do they ask for, what do they actually want, what's the friction?Surfaces whether data-sharing norms already exist in their industry — that changes everything about what we can build on top.
  3. What's been your experience with sensors in your operation? What's worked, what's failed, what made you stop?BroodMinder-style sensors have a long track record of beekeeper rejection. Why — labor? failure rate? data trust? — determines whether our approach can succeed where they didn't.
  4. If a neutral platform existed that deployed sensors across your operation at no cost to you, produced continuous hive-performance reports, and could serve both you and your pollination clients — what would you need to see in return to participate? Free sensors? Data revenue share? Ownership?Don't offer terms. Ask what they'd need. Their answer tells you the feasible business model.
  5. Would you be willing to share raw hive telemetry with a grower, a CPG buyer, or an insurance underwriter — through a neutral platform that controls access? What conditions would make that yes, what makes it no?The hardest question. This is where "I'll share with anyone" becomes "I'll never share raw data with an insurer." Do not skip.
  6. If the 2024–25 colony losses happened again next year, what would you want to exist that doesn't exist today — data, insurance, contracts, anything?Opens the disaster-coping mindset. Their answer often reveals the product nobody has named yet.
  7. Honest question: is there any version of an instrumented-hive platform owned by a third party that you would participate in? If no, what's the real blocker?Kill question. If three of three say "absolutely not under any terms," the upstream layer is broken and no downstream demand saves it.

Scoring rubric · beekeeper axis

Strong signal · pass
2+ operators say yes to Q4/Q5 with specific, feasible terms (free hardware + data ownership + shared reporting, or similar). At least one volunteers a pilot.
Weak signal · conditional
1+ operator says yes conditionally; others say "maybe" but want to see a reference customer first. Means we need one anchor operator, which becomes the critical path.
Kill · axis closed
3/3 answer Q7 with "we would never share raw data with insurers/brands regardless of terms." Thesis broken upstream; consider a grower-first model instead of a beekeeper-first model.

Honesty about "we're not building our own classifier" is disarming. Vendors are more open when you concede your limits and name them as the expert.

Diagnostic script · 20 minutes · acoustic vendor

  1. Walk me through your current data product — what do you actually sell? Raw audio features, species classifications, diversity indices, full dashboard?Gets the abstraction level right. We care about mid-level features we can integrate, not finished reports that compete with ours.
  2. Who currently licenses your data, and at what structure — per-site, per-API, annual license, per-report?Their answer is our unit economics constraint.
  3. What's your US deployment footprint today? If we sold to a US customer, would your hardware be feasible to deploy — reps, installation, support?Critical logistic question. UK-built hardware without US support is a landmine.
  4. Who owns the raw site data — you, the landowner, or the licensee? Is it exclusive or non-exclusive?Determines whether we can re-license to insurers and consultancies, or whether we're boxed in.
  5. Would a white-label / OEM arrangement be on the table, where your hardware ships under our brand for specific customers?Worth asking. They'll almost certainly say "not today" but the shape of the no is informative.
  6. Is there a fundamental reason you wouldn't license your data stream to us on any terms? If yes, what is it — competitive, IP, technical?Kill question. Both vendors refusing on IP/competitive grounds ends the thesis in its integrated form.

Diagnostic script · 20 minutes · methodology shop (Plan Vivo / Verra)

  1. Are any pilot methodologies for pollinator-outcome credits or pollination-ecosystem-service credits currently in development or review at your organization?Baseline. If nothing is in the pipeline, the nature-credits buyer is 3+ years further out than we assumed.
  2. For a pollination-specific claim or credit, what landscape-level data would need to be collected — native pollinator species presence, habitat health metrics, floral resource continuity, yield outcome, hive-level stress?Defines the minimum viable data schema.
  3. Who is currently supplying data in pilot methodologies that exist? Is the data layer owned by credit issuers, developers, or independent vendors?Tells us if there's a vendor slot or if it's all vertically integrated.
  4. What would disqualify a dataset from being usable — unverified species identification, sampling gaps, lack of peer review, proprietary methods?The rigor bar we'd have to clear to be credit-grade.
  5. Realistic horizon: when do you expect the first pollinator-outcome credits to actually be issued and transacted? 2027? 2029? 2032?Kill-ish. If the honest answer is "2030+," this buyer is out of the Phase 0 frame and we shouldn't plan runway around it.

Week 5–6 · Synthesis 05
Founder memo · go / pivot / kill

The decision. Write the memo, present to the board, commit.

Twelve calls produce a lot of noise. The purpose of week six is to force the noise into one decision, in writing, without rationalization. Use the framework below.
Memo structure

Length · 4–6 pages. Shorter than the research memos, more decisive. A board reads 5 pages carefully; they skim 15.

Sections:

  • Rubric scoreboard. For each of the four axes, one sentence: signal band (strong / weak / kill) and one-line rationale drawn from call notes. No narrative. Just the scoreboard.Reader absorbs in 15 seconds. Anchors the rest of the memo in evidence.
  • What we learned that we did not expect. Every Phase 0 surfaces two or three findings that were not in the plan. Name them. They are the most valuable part of the sprint.Signals integrity. A memo with zero surprises is a memo that wasn't honest with itself.
  • What changed in the thesis. If everything held, say so. If the wedge narrowed, say which layer survived and which didn't. If the wedge expanded, say which new buyer emerged.Boards reward specificity about thesis evolution far more than they reward defending the original plan.
  • Verdict. One of three — go, pivot, kill. Named commitment.The word "go" must appear, or "pivot to X," or "return capital." No hedged verdicts.
  • If go: the 6-month MVP plan. What we build, with whom, for whom, with what budget. Named anchor buyer (from Phase 0 conversations). Three milestones with dates.The board needs to approve the next stage; give them the specifics they need to vote yes.
  • If pivot: the narrower bet + new kill conditions. Same rigor, smaller scope.
  • If kill: honest capital-return calculation and team next-step. What's left in the bank, what the honest residual is, what the team does next.The hardest section to write. Write it first if you think it's the answer — writing the kill memo first protects you from rationalizing your way out of it.
Verdict · go
Commit to 6-month MVP build
Trigger: 3+ axes returned strong signal with at least one in insurance OR consultancy. Named anchor buyer identified. Team alignment on value-first framing.
Verdict · pivot
Narrow to surviving axis
Trigger: 1–2 axes returned strong signal; others weak or kill. Rewrite thesis around the surviving layer only. New Phase 0 on the narrowed bet, 4 weeks, $3K.
Verdict · kill
Return capital or restart
Trigger: 3+ axes returned kill signal. Thesis dead. Board reconvene within 14 days to decide: return capital, or run a value-first restart on a different market entirely.
Phase 0 hygiene !
Anti-patterns to avoid

Six ways Phase 0 quietly fails.

These are the traps most founder-led validation sprints fall into. None of them produce an obvious failure — they produce a warm, plausible-sounding memo that isn't evidence. Re-read this section at the end of week three and week five.
Do not do
  • Pitching instead of diagnosing.
    The moment you start describing a feature set, you are in sales mode. They will be polite. You will get noise, not signal. If you hear yourself explaining the product for more than 90 seconds, stop and ask a question.
  • Skipping the kill question.
    Because the conversation was warm. Because they said some nice things. The kill question exists precisely to cut through niceness. Ask it every time, without exception.
  • Interpreting "interesting" as "yes."
    "That's interesting, keep me posted" is always "no" until it comes with a follow-up meeting, an intro, or money. Log it as weak signal, not strong.
  • Filling in gaps with imagination.
    If a contact doesn't answer a question directly, don't reconstruct what they "probably" meant. Note it unanswered. Ask the next contact. Three unanswered instances of the same question is itself a signal.
  • Believing the first "no" more than the fifth "yes" — or vice versa.
    Rubrics exist to calibrate. One unusually negative call can be one bad personality match; one unusually positive call can be one outlier enthusiast. Trust the pattern, not the peaks.
  • Writing the memo last.
    Start the memo in week three. Add notes as calls happen. By week six, the synthesis is half-written and far more honest — because the early thoughts haven't been filtered through hindsight bias.
