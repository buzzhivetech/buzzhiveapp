BuzzHive outreach guide

We're doing a short industry-research project at BuzzHive over the next week. We want to interview about twelve people across four industries (insurance, beekeepers, acoustic-sensor companies, and biodiversity consultants) to understand how they work and what they'd find useful. Your job is to help us schedule those interviews, take notes, and keep everything organized. This guide has everything you need.

Section 01

Your job, in five steps

This is the whole sprint in five moves. Come back to this section whenever you're not sure what to do next.

  1. Send outreach emails from the contact list
    Use the template in Section 03. Personalize lightly. Aim to send 3–5 emails per week. Keep a tracker (Section 07).
  2. Follow up after 5 business days if no response
    One gentle follow-up. No more than that. If still no response after a second 5-day wait, mark as "no response" in the tracker and move on.
  3. Schedule the call and send a calendar invite
    20-minute slots, Zoom or phone. Send the invite within 24 hours of them saying yes. Include the Zoom link and a one-line agenda ("short informational interview for a research project").
  4. Join the call and take notes — or lead it, depending
    Some calls a BuzzHive exec leads and you take notes. Some you lead yourself. The contact list (Section 02) tells you which is which. Either way, fill in the call-log form afterward (Section 06).
  5. Send a thank-you within 24 hours
    Short, sincere, one paragraph. Makes the difference if we need to follow up later.
Important · this is research, not sales
You are not selling anything. You are asking questions. If someone asks what BuzzHive is planning to build, say: "Honestly, we're still figuring that out — these conversations are part of how we decide." That's the right answer. Don't improvise beyond it. If they press, offer to loop in a BuzzHive exec for a follow-up call.

Section 04

What to ask on the call

Five simple questions per group. Ask them in order, in plain language. If they get off-track, let them — then come back to the list. You don't need to cover every question if time runs short; Q1, Q3, and Q5 are the most important in every group.

Group 1 · Parametric insurance & reinsurers
  1. Can you walk me through how your team thinks about agriculture parametric products today — what perils you cover, what data triggers them?
  2. Has pollination failure or bee colony loss ever come up as something worth insuring? Why or why not?
  3. If a dataset existed that combined hive health data, native pollinator data, and landscape data — would that be something your team would potentially use, partner on, or not be relevant?
  4. What does data need to look like to be "insurance-grade" — peer-reviewed methodology, years of history, something else?
  5. Honestly, is there any version of bee or pollination data your team might pay for in the next two years? If no, what would have to change for it to become yes?
The exec will lead these calls. Your job is to take detailed notes — verbatim if you can, otherwise paraphrased with quotes where possible. Focus on the answer to Q5; that's the single most important response.
Group 2 · Commercial beekeepers
  1. Can you walk me through your operation — how many hives, what do you focus on (pollination, honey, queens), what's your contract structure with growers?
  2. What data, if any, do you currently share with growers? What do they ask for, and what's the friction?
  3. What's been your experience with sensor systems in your operation — what's worked, what hasn't, why?
  4. If a platform existed that put sensors on your hives at no cost to you and produced continuous hive-performance reports, what would you want in return to participate?
  5. Would you ever be willing to share hive data with a grower, a food brand, or an insurance underwriter — through a neutral platform? What would make that yes or no?
You lead these calls. Beekeepers are patient and candid — let them tell stories. Q5 is the most sensitive question; save it for the second half of the call after rapport is built.
Group 3 · Acoustic-sensor companies & nature-credit orgs
  1. Acoustic vendors: What do you actually sell — raw audio, species classifications, dashboards? And who are your typical customers?
  2. Acoustic vendors: How do you structure data licensing — per-site, per-API, annual license?
  3. Acoustic vendors: Would you be open to licensing your data to a platform that integrates it with other pollination data streams? Why or why not?
  4. Nature-credit orgs: Are any pilot methodologies for pollinator-related credits currently in development at your organization? What data do they require?
  5. Nature-credit orgs: Realistically, when do you expect the first pollinator-outcome credits to actually be issued and transacted — 2027? 2030? Later?
You lead these calls. These are the most technical — ask them to slow down if they use acronyms you don't know. Write the acronyms down and ask what each means before the end of the call.
Group 4 · Biodiversity consultants
  1. Can you walk me through your workflow at a real client site — what do you collect, how, and from which vendors?
  2. Where does the time go in a typical project — sampling, integrating data, writing reports, client management?
  3. If a platform existed that deployed at your client sites and produced continuous data under your brand, would that be something you'd use?
  4. What would you want to pay, and in what structure — per site, per project, annual subscription?
  5. Would you be open to piloting something like this at one client site, for free, in exchange for feedback?
The exec will lead this one. Your job is to take notes and send the calendar invite on time. These are the most valuable calls we'll run — don't let the invite slip.

Section 07

If you get stuck

Common situations and what to do. If something comes up that isn't in here, ping the team in Slack — don't guess.

They reply and ask what BuzzHive does before agreeing to a call.
Keep it short: "BuzzHive is building technology for beekeepers and pollination. We're doing industry research right now to figure out what to build next. That's what I'd love to talk to you about." Don't elaborate on products or strategy — you genuinely don't have the details yet.
They redirect you to someone else on their team.
Thank them, add the new person to the tracker, and send the new person the same outreach template with a note: "Hi [new person], [first contact] suggested I reach out to you about [their industry] research. Would you be open to a 20-minute call?" That referral makes the new outreach ~3x more likely to land.
They ask what specific questions you'll ask.
Share the five questions from Section 04 directly. There's nothing secret about them. People are much more likely to say yes when they know exactly what's being asked.
They ghost after agreeing to a call.
One gentle nudge: "Hi [first name], still hoping to find 20 minutes if your calendar opens up — no pressure." If still no response a week later, mark as "no response" and move on.
They're clearly hostile or think this is a sales call.
Thank them politely, end the outreach, don't follow up. "Completely understand — I appreciate you letting me know. Wishing you the best." Mark as "declined" in the tracker.
You get a call offered during a timeslot an exec needs to join.
Default to accepting the slot they offered — never make them reschedule. If the exec who needs to join can't make it, you take notes solo and flag which questions the exec wants emphasized beforehand.
You don't understand what someone said in a call.
Ask. "Can you explain that one more time, I'm new to this side of the industry." Every person you're talking to will be flattered to explain — and the clarification is worth much more than a smart-sounding summary you're unsure about.
You're not sure if something you heard matters.
Write it down anyway. We'll sort importance later. The call-log form has an "anything surprising?" field — use it generously. A casual throwaway comment from one contact can end up being the most valuable thing we learn.
The finish line

When this is done · what success looks like

You'll know the sprint is a success if, by the end of six weeks, the shared folder has twelve call-log files, the tracker shows twelve rows marked "done," and the BuzzHive team has the information they need to decide what to build. The quality of your notes and the consistency of your outreach is what makes that possible.

Thank you for running this. It matters a lot.
